“Art of the Web” Reading Responses

Week 3 — Laurel Schwulst — My website is a shifting house next to a river of knowledge. What could yours be from Creative Independent & Are.na’s How Do You Use the Internet Mindfully

In “My website is a shifting house next to a river of knowledge,” Laurel Schwulst argues that the internet should be understood as a place we inhabit rather than a tool we use, and that personal websites are essential acts of care within an increasingly commercialized web. She reframes websites as living, unfinished environments: rooms, gardens, shelves, puddles, or even temporary gestures, whose form is shaped by metaphor, intention, and time. By contrasting personal websites with corporate social media platforms, Schwulst emphasizes autonomy, authorship, and world-building, positioning website-making as both a creative and ethical practice. Artists and individuals, Schwulst argues, are uniquely capable of shaping a more humane web by designing spaces that evolve with their ideas, resist optimization, and contribute to the digital commons rather than extract from it.

Q1: If personal websites are meant to be slow, unfinished, and resistant to optimization, how can they meaningfully function within an internet ecosystem driven by visibility, commerciality, and speed?

Q2: What responsibilities do designers and artists have when shaping digital spaces that others will inhabit, especially when those spaces influence identity, behavior, and care over time?

Week 4 — Olia Lialina — STILL THERE Ruins and Templates of Geocities from Lost and Living (in) Archives

It is counterintuitive to think of the web as something that can age or be forgotten, yet the mass deletion of GeoCities by Yahoo! reveals how fragile distributed digital memory truly is. The broken images, outdated layouts, and abandoned pages left behind function like architectural ruins, traces of past ways of living, designing, and communicating online. I believe that while visual styles often return through cycles of nostalgia and reinterpretation, what truly becomes obsolete is technique: the infrastructures and technical systems that no longer function once platforms disappear. Seen this way, digital ruins are not merely failures, but historical artifacts that gain new meaning as they are re-read and reinterpreted within changing cultural contexts.

Q1: If digital spaces can age, decay, and disappear like buildings, who is responsible for maintaining, preserving, or letting them fade, and according to whose values?

Q2: If digital ruins gain meaning through reinterpretation rather than functionality, how might designers today intentionally design for future decay or reinterpretation on the web?

Week 5 — Marisa Olson — POSTINTERNET art after the internet

Marisa Olson describes a post-internet world where the internet is no longer a separate space but something that quietly shapes how art is made, shared, and experienced. Instead of focusing on art made online, she explains that artists now create within a culture defined by image circulation, global connectivity, and digital reproduction, even when their work exists physically. What resonates with me most is the idea that the internet has become a background condition rather than just a tool. It made me think about how we often encounter art, architecture, and places as images before we experience them in real life, and how that changes the way we design, value, and understand the physical world.

Q1: If we already live in a post-internet condition, can any artwork today truly exist outside of network culture?

Q2: How does constant image circulation online change the way we judge authenticity and originality?

Week 7 — Bojana Coklyat & Shannon Finnegan — Selections from Alt-Text as Poetry Workbook

Alt Text as Poetry Workbook invites us to see alt text not just as a technical requirement but as a thoughtful and creative act of care. The authors explore how describing images for blind and low vision audiences is both an accessibility responsibility and a form of translation that involves choice, perspective, and language. Drawing from poetry, they encourage attention to tone, brevity, and experimentation, while acknowledging that there is no single objective way to describe an image. Ultimately, the workbook reminds us that strong alt text centers disabled communities and treats accessibility as an ongoing, collaborative, and ethical practice rather than an afterthought.

Q1: Should alt-text be considered part of the artwork itself?

Q2: Does alt-text challenge the idea that vision is the primary way we understand the world?

Week 8 — Ramsey Nasser — A Personal Computer for Children of All Cultures_ from Decolonising the Digital

In “A Personal Computer for Children of All Cultures,” Ramsey Nasser argues that computers and programming are not culturally neutral because modern software systems are deeply rooted in the English language and Western technological history. I found the reading compelling because it reveals that technology carries cultural and political assumptions rather than being purely objective. It also connects to broader issues of globalization, where systems claim universality while still privileging certain cultures. Two questions the reading raises for me are:

Q1: Is a truly multilingual and culturally equal computing system possible?

Q2: How much cultural identity is lost when global technological standards prioritize efficiency over diversity?

Week 10 — Silvio Lorusso — Selections from What Design Can’t Do

In “Form Follows Format,” Silvio Lorusso argues that digital tools, templates, platforms, and automation increasingly determine the structure of design, weakening the exclusive authority of professional designers and shifting their role from makers of form to interpreters and managers of semi-automated systems. In my opinion, The advancement of AI seems inevitable, so instead of resisting automation, designers should learn how to harness it intelligently. There are still aspects of design that AI cannot replace, especially the intellect of the hand, where ideas evolve through sketching, tracing, and iterative exploration on paper. In this tactile process, designers search for better aesthetics and deeper design philosophy. AI and automation can support this process by helping with brainstorming and generating possibilities, but the designer’s human judgment should always guide the final decisions.

Q1: If design formats are increasingly determined by platforms and algorithms, how can designers maintain genuine creative authorship within these systems?

Q2: If anyone can design using accessible tools, what distinguishes the role of a professional designer today?

Week 12 — Dot Tuer — Beyond the New Media Frame The Poetics of Absence in Vera Frenkel’s String Games

What I found compelling is how the article challenges the assumption that technology brings people closer together. It made me think about how even today, constant connection does not necessarily mean real presence. That insight feels sharp and still very relevant. At the same time, I feel the argument leans a bit too heavily on the idea of absence. While that reading is strong, it starts to dominate everything else. The work itself seems experimental and open ended, even playful, but the article frames it mostly through loss and memory, which narrows how we experience it.

Q1: Has technology changed what we mean by “being present”?

Q2: Can gaps in communication create new possibilities instead of just loss?

Week 13 — Paul Soulellis — Performing the Feed

What I found most interesting in Soulellis’ argument is how the feed changes publishing into something we don’t really read anymore, but something we simply exist within, almost like a background atmosphere. It feels unsettling because what seems like a smooth and neutral flow is actually shaped by hidden algorithms and power structures, which makes it harder to know what is real or intentional. It makes me think that the challenge today is not just getting information, but learning how to slow down and really see it, and that creating moments of pause or interruption might be a meaningful way to push back against that constant stream.

Q1: If we are constantly immersed in endless feeds, how can we train ourselves to recognize what is meaningful rather than just reacting to what is most visible or immediate?

Q2: As algorithms increasingly shape what we see and how we understand information, who should be responsible for ensuring transparency and accountability in these systems?

Week 14 — Henry Desroches — A website to destroy all websites

The reading argues that the internet has evolved from a creative, user-driven space into a profit-driven system that limits autonomy. What really stayed with me is how familiar this feeling is: the idea that the internet used to feel more open and personal, and now it often feels exhausting or empty. I don’t think it’s just nostalgia; it actually matches how we interact with it today, constantly pulled by feeds and algorithms. At the same time, I’m not sure how easy it is to go back. Even if personal websites sound ideal, most people stay on big platforms because they’re convenient and where everyone already is. It makes me wonder if the issue isn’t just the tools, but how deeply our habits and expectations have already adapted to them.

Q1: If we’re so used to fast, algorithm-driven apps, what would actually make people want to use slower, more personal websites again?

Q2: Even if we build a more open and independent internet, would it eventually end up becoming controlled and centralized again?

Week 15 — Boris Groys — Art on the Internet

Groys’ essay made me rethink the Internet not just as a platform for sharing art, but as something that actually changes what art is. What stood out most is how art online loses its sense of distance and fiction—it just sits alongside everything else as information, tied to the real identity of the artist. At the same time, the Internet makes artists constantly visible, which feels both freeing and uncomfortable. It removes the private space where creative work used to develop, turning process into something exposed and tracked. That tension between openness and surveillance feels especially relevant today, where being seen is necessary but also limiting.

Q1: Can real creativity still happen without privacy and distance from the public eye?

Q2: Does the Internet make artists more defined by their identity, or does it reduce them to just another stream of data?
